# Chest Pain or Pressure: Call 911 or Book a Visit?

It happens more often than people think. Someone feels a heavy weight in the chest while unloading materials at a job site or after a big dinner, sits down for a while, tells themselves "it's probably nothing" and waits for it to go away. Others get scared by a two-second twinge when they roll over in bed. Somewhere between those two reactions is a question worth answering before the moment comes: is this an ambulance situation or a clinic visit?

This guide doesn't replace an exam, but it does give you a clear rule for deciding fast. And the rule starts with what can't wait.

## The non-negotiable part: when to dial 911

Call 911 right away if chest discomfort comes with any of these signs:

- Pain, pressure, tightness or a squeezing feeling in the chest, especially if it's strong, lasts more than a few minutes, or fades and comes back.
- Pain that spreads to one or both arms, the shoulder, the back, the neck or the jaw.
- Shortness of breath, even if the pain itself isn't intense.
- A cold sweat, nausea or vomiting, strong dizziness, or feeling like you're about to pass out.
- One side of the face drooping, a weak arm or leg, or slurred or garbled speech: that points to a stroke, and it's a 911 call too.

In any of these cases, **don't drive yourself**, and don't ask a relative to race you to the hospital either. Don't come to the clinic, and don't wait to see if it passes. Paramedics can start treating you the moment they reach your door, they alert the hospital while on the road, and they carry the equipment to act if the heart falls out of rhythm. During a heart attack, every minute matters for saving heart muscle.

### While the ambulance is on its way

- Sit or lie down in a comfortable position and stop any physical effort.
- Unlock the front door, or have someone wait outside to wave the paramedics in.
- Keep your medication list handy, or gather the bottles in a bag.
- Don't take anything on your own: follow only what the 911 dispatcher tells you over the phone.

## When the signs are quieter

Not every heart attack looks like the movies, with someone clutching their chest. General guidance from the American Heart Association points out that women, older adults and people living with diabetes sometimes feel something less obvious: an odd tiredness that doesn't fit the day, getting winded walking distances that used to be easy, discomfort in the upper stomach, back or jaw pain, or nausea with no clear reason.

If you or someone at home has any of these symptoms and they're new, strong or won't let up, treat them the same way: 911. It's far better to hear at the hospital that it wasn't your heart than to stay home wondering.

## Chest discomfort that often isn't the heart

The chest holds ribs, muscles, lungs, the esophagus and the top of the stomach, and almost any of them can hurt. Some common culprits:

- **Muscles or ribs:** after lifting, a bump or several days of coughing. It usually hurts when you press on one specific spot, twist your torso or take a deep breath.
- **Heartburn or reflux:** a burning feeling that rises behind the breastbone after spicy, fried or large meals, or when you lie down on a full stomach; sometimes it leaves a sour taste in your mouth.
- **Respiratory infections:** bronchitis or a bad bout of flu can leave the chest sore and congested, often with fever or a cough.
- **Stress and anxiety:** the heart speeds up, breathing turns shallow and a tightness shows up that can be very frightening.

An important warning here: nobody can be sure of the cause from their living room. A heart attack can also feel like "just heartburn," and anxiety can mimic a heart problem. If the discomfort is strong, new, or comes with any of the warning signs listed above, the decision is already made: 911.

## When a clinic visit makes sense

A visit to the clinic is for what isn't an emergency right now. For example:

- You had mild chest discomfort a few days ago, it's completely gone, it didn't come with shortness of breath, sweating or nausea, and you want to know what it was.
- You have a twinge that hurts when you press on it with a finger or when you move, after heavy lifting or a fall.
- You get a burning chest after certain meals, over and over.
- You've been dragging a cough and chest soreness since a cold or bronchitis; the [respiratory illness](/services/enfermedades-respiratorias) visit is the right fit for that.
- Your blood pressure came out high at a pharmacy kiosk or at work, or you live with high blood pressure, diabetes or high cholesterol and haven't been checked in a while: that's handled in [chronic conditions](/services/condiciones-cronicas) follow-up.
- You were asked for an electrocardiogram for work, sports or before surgery, or your heartbeat sometimes feels like it skips: the clinic does the [electrocardiogram (EKG)](/services/electrocardiograma) in a few minutes, with no needles.

If the discomfort comes back hard while you're waiting your turn at the clinic, tell the front desk at that very moment.

## What the visit covers

The visit starts with listening. The clinic's medical team will ask what the discomfort felt like, how long it lasted, what you were doing and what eased it. Then, depending on what you share, it may include:

- A resting blood pressure and pulse reading, plus a check of your oxygen level.
- Listening to your heart and lungs, and checking your chest for a tender spot in the muscles or ribs.
- An EKG, if your case calls for it, to look at your heart's rhythm.
- [Blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) such as glucose and cholesterol, when they help clarify your risks.

With that information you'll talk through the next step: a plan for reflux, care for a strained muscle, blood pressure control or, if something warrants it, a specialized evaluation or a transfer to the ER. If you leave with a prescription, you buy it right there at the clinic's [pharmacy](/services/farmacia), with no extra stop on the way home. And if you already take medicine for blood pressure or your heart, don't stop it or switch it on your own: talk with the medical team before changing anything.

## What to note on your phone

If the discomfort has passed and you're booking a visit, the more precise you are, the more you get out of it. Write down:

1. The day and time it started, and how many minutes it lasted.
2. What you were doing: lifting, walking, eating, lying down or resting.
3. Exactly where you felt it and whether it moved to another part of your body.
4. Whether it was burning, stabbing, heavy or squeezing.
5. What made it better or worse: deep breaths, moving, eating, resting.
6. Whether anyone in your family had a heart attack or died suddenly at a young age.

## Before it happens again

One easy rule for the whole household: strong or new discomfort, or any with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea or pain that travels to the arm or jaw, means 911, and nobody gets behind the wheel. Mild discomfort that's already gone, burning that keeps coming back with meals, or a blood pressure number that needs watching means a clinic visit.

If you live in Aldine or near 77037, the Airline Drive clinic sees patients in order of arrival from 9 AM to 9 PM. For a blood pressure check, bring the notebook with your home readings and your pill bottles: with those two things in hand, the visit gets off to a head start.
