# Pap Smear: What It Finds, How Often and How to Prepare

"When was your last Pap smear?" Few questions in the exam room get a longer pause. Some women think it was two years ago when it was really six; others had one back home and never heard the result; others figure that since nothing hurts, they can skip it. This guide answers three practical questions: what the test picks up, how often you need it and what to do in the days before so the sample works the first time.

## What a Pap smear looks for

The cervix is the lower end of the uterus, the part that sits at the top of the vagina. Its cells renew constantly, and now and then some start growing in an unusual shape. A Pap smear collects a small sample of those cells so the lab can look at them under a microscope.

What it is looking for are those changes in the cells of the cervix, before they cause any symptoms. Most of the time they are mild changes the body clears on its own; other times they need closer follow-up or treatment. That is the real strength of the test: it spots the problem years before it could turn into cancer, while the woman still feels completely fine.

Behind nearly all of those changes is HPV (human papillomavirus), a very common virus that many people carry at some point without ever knowing. That is why, past a certain age, the Pap test may be paired with an HPV test taken during the same visit.

## What a Pap smear does not check

This is where a lot of mix-ups happen. A normal Pap result does not mean everything has been looked at:

- **It does not check the ovaries.** To see the uterus and ovaries from the inside there is the [pelvic ultrasound](/services/ultrasonido), ordered when there is pain or unusual bleeding.
- **It does not replace a mammogram.** Breasts are checked separately; at the gynecology visit you can get a mammogram order when your case calls for it.
- **It does not confirm a pregnancy.** If your period is late, what you need is a [pregnancy test](/services/prueba-embarazo).
- **It does not, by itself, explain smelly discharge or itching.** For that a vaginal culture is taken, and it can be done at the same visit.

If you have any of those symptoms, say so when you arrive: that way the exam covers everything you need in one go.

## How often you need it

General medical recommendations change with your age and your past results. Use this table as a rough guide; your exact schedule is set during the visit:

| Age or situation | General guidance |
| --- | --- |
| Under 21 | Usually not done, even if you are already sexually active |
| 21 to 29 | A Pap smear every 3 years if results come back normal |
| 30 to 65 | Every 3 years with a Pap alone, or every 5 years when paired with an HPV test |
| Over 65 | You may be able to stop if your results in recent years were normal |
| No uterus after surgery | Depends on why you had the operation; bring that detail to the visit |

Some situations shorten those intervals: an abnormal result in the past, earlier treatment on the cervix, or a weakened immune system from an illness or a medicine. One more point: having had the HPV vaccine does not take away the need for screening.

If you can't remember the date of your last Pap, that's fine. It is actually the best reason to do it now and start counting again with a result in hand.

## How to prepare in five steps

The sample is small, and anything that coats the cells can make it harder to read. So getting ready starts before you leave home.

**When picking the date.** Work out when your next period is due and choose a day well away from it, ideally mid-cycle. If your period shows up that very day, it's better to reschedule; light spotting can be mentioned at check-in, and the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz decides whether the sample can be taken.

**Two days before.** No douching or internal rinses, no vaginal suppositories, creams, gels or lubricants, and no intercourse. A regular shower is perfectly fine.

**The night before.** Save three things on your phone: the first day of your latest period, roughly what year your previous Pap was, and whether you were ever told one came back abnormal. If you had it done in another country, any paperwork you kept helps.

**On the day.** Wear something comfortable, ideally a skirt or pants that are easy to slip off. Empty your bladder before going into the exam room; it makes the exam feel more relaxed.

**At check-in.** Mention it if you are pregnant or think you could be, if you gave birth recently, or if you bleed after sex. None of that is anything to be embarrassed about, and all of it changes how the sample is read.

## What happens in the exam room

You lie on the table with a sheet over you and your feet resting in place. A speculum gently holds the vaginal walls apart so the cervix can be seen, and a small soft brush picks up a few cells. Most women feel pressure or a short moment of discomfort. Breathing deeply and letting your belly go loose helps, and you can ask to pause at any time.

The whole thing takes just a few minutes. If there is discharge or itching, the culture is collected during that same exam.

## When the result comes in

The sample is processed by the lab, and you are contacted as soon as the result is ready. There are three possible scenarios:

1. **Normal.** You come back on the schedule that fits your age.
2. **Mild changes, or a sample that couldn't be read well.** Often it is enough to repeat the test later or add the HPV test.
3. **Changes that need a closer look.** If the result is abnormal, a specialized evaluation is a good idea so the cervix can be examined in more detail.

An abnormal Pap is not a cancer diagnosis. It is an early warning, which is exactly what the test is for.

## When not to wait for a routine visit

Some symptoms are not a Pap smear matter and need urgent care. These signs mean dialing 911 or heading straight to a hospital:

- Very heavy bleeding with dizziness or fainting
- Sudden, severe pelvic pain
- Being pregnant, or possibly pregnant, while bleeding or feeling sharp pain low on one side
- High fever together with pelvic pain
