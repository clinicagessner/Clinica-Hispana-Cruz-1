# Pelvic Ultrasound: Pain, Irregular Periods, What It Shows

Plenty of women put up with certain symptoms for months, telling themselves it's probably nothing. A sharp twinge on the right side that comes back every month. A period that shows up after three weeks, then not again for two months. Spotting between periods that no one has explained. When one of these finally makes it into the exam room, the next step is often to take a look inside, and that's what a pelvic ultrasound is for: a scan with no needles and no radiation that shows what sits below your belly button.

Here the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz walks through what this scan checks, when it tends to be ordered, how to show up ready and what happens if the picture raises a new question.

## Three women, one scan

Every patient is different, yet a few stories keep coming up:

- **She works on her feet all day** and feels a dull ache low in her belly that gets worse by the end of her shift or during her period. She has been taking pain pills on her own for a while, and they don't help the way they used to.
- **She can't predict her period anymore.** Some months it's right on time, others it skips, and when it comes it lasts longer or brings clots. She wants to know whether something changed in her uterus or ovaries.
- **She left another visit with an open question:** someone once mentioned a possible ovarian cyst, but she never had the scan to confirm it or follow up.

In all three cases, talking with the medical team comes first. The ultrasound is added when your story and the physical exam suggest an image will actually help.

## What shows up on the monitor

The clinic's [ultrasound](/services/ultrasonido) page lists three organs for the pelvic scan:

- **The uterus:** its size and shape, plus how its wall and inner lining look. Generally speaking, ultrasound is one of the usual ways to look for fibroids (muscle growths that are almost always benign) and other changes that may explain heavy periods or pain.
- **The ovaries:** their size and whether there are structures such as cysts. Many cysts are functional, meaning they form with your cycle and go away by themselves; the image helps decide whether watching is enough or another step makes sense.
- **The bladder:** seen full, it works as a window onto the organs behind it, and its shape or wall can also be noted if anything stands out.

As the scan goes on, you hear in Spanish or English what is on the screen and why it matters. An ultrasound doesn't hand out labels on its own: what appears is read alongside your symptoms, your age and your history.

## When it tends to be ordered

The clinic's page names two main reasons: pelvic pain and irregular bleeding. Day to day, that covers situations like:

- Pain low in the abdomen or on one side that keeps coming back or won't go away.
- Periods that change rhythm, get much heavier or last noticeably longer than usual for you.
- Bleeding between periods or after sex.
- A suspected ovarian cyst, or one seen before that needs a second look.
- Pressure or bladder discomfort that a urine test alone doesn't explain.

If you also have burning when you pee or feel like you have to go constantly, the medical team may first check for a [urinary tract infection](/services/infecciones-urinarias), which is confirmed with a urine sample.

## The full bladder: the step people forget

A pelvic scan calls for a full bladder. The reason is simple: water in the bladder gently nudges the intestines aside and lets sound waves travel clearly to the uterus and ovaries. With an empty bladder the picture gets blurry, and sometimes you end up drinking more water and waiting.

To arrive ready:

1. Drink several glasses of water before leaving home, timing it to your drive along Airline Drive.
2. Hold it until the scan is over, even if the last stretch is uncomfortable.
3. If you're not sure how much to drink, message the clinic on WhatsApp before coming in, as its ultrasound page suggests, so the trip isn't wasted.

No fasting is needed for this one, unlike an abdominal ultrasound.

## How it feels on the table

You lie on your back with your lower belly uncovered. A warm gel goes on your skin, and a small handheld wand called a transducer glides over it, sending images to the monitor. With a full bladder the pressure feels stronger and may give you an urgent need to pee, but it isn't painful. Most scans wrap up quickly, and you can get back to your day as soon as the gel is wiped off.

## Where it fits with your gynecology visit

A pelvic ultrasound is rarely a stand-alone event. It often grows out of a [gynecology](/services/ginecologia) visit, which covers the routine exam, the Pap smear and, when there's unusual discharge or itching, a vaginal culture. If that conversation turns up recurring pain or unpredictable periods, the ultrasound fills in what a physical exam can't show.

If you'd rather handle everything in one trip, the clinic offers the **Women's Checkup with Ultrasound**, which bundles the pelvic ultrasound, a Pap smear, a urine test and the medical consultation. Package details are on the [promotions](/promociones) page.

If your period is late and pregnancy is possible, start with a [pregnancy test](/services/prueba-embarazo): the answer changes which scan makes sense and how it's done.

## When the picture calls for more

Sometimes a pelvic ultrasound settles everything; sometimes it opens a new question. The medical team explains the findings and what they mean for your health. If your case needs another test, treatment or a different study the clinic doesn't perform, the team guides the referral to a specialist and tells you what comes next, so you're not left holding an image with no answers.

Some findings only become clear with time. A functional cyst, for example, is usually rechecked after one or two cycles to see whether it has gone away.

## Skip the wait and get emergency care if...

Some pain doesn't belong in a scheduled visit. Head to the ER or call 911 if you have:

- Sudden, severe pain on one side of your belly, especially with nausea, vomiting that won't stop or fainting.
- Bleeding heavy enough to soak through a pad in an hour or less.
- A high fever along with pelvic pain.
- Pain or bleeding when you think you might be pregnant.

## Before you leave the house

Three things make the scan more useful:

- The first day of your last period and, if you can, the dates from the past three or four months saved on your phone.
- Any earlier ultrasound or imaging report, even from another country.
- The name of your birth control method, if you use one, since some change your bleeding pattern.
