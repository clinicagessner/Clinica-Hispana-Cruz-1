# Weak Stream, Night Trips to Pee: Prostate After 40

It rarely shows up all at once. At first it's one trip to the bathroom in the middle of the night, then two, then three. The stream takes a while to get going, comes out weaker than it used to, and a few drops still escape after you've zipped up. Plenty of men around Aldine shrug it off as "just getting older" and live with it for years, especially when they spend the day behind the wheel of a truck or work long shifts with no restroom nearby. These symptoms have real, usually treatable causes, though, and it's worth getting them checked before they turn into something bigger.

Here the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz covers the usual causes, what a basic urology visit involves, and when to skip the clinic and go to the ER.

## Signs men tend to brush off

The symptoms can be sorted by when they show up:

- **While emptying:** a thin stream, or one that stops and starts, having to push to get it flowing, standing at the toilet waiting for it to begin, and dribbling that continues even after you shake off.
- **While filling:** sudden urges you can't put off long enough to finish a phone call, heading to the restroom every hour during your shift, and waking up two or more times a night with a full bladder (known as nocturia).
- **Afterward:** the feeling that something is still in there and needing to go back a few minutes later.

What helps most is knowing when it started, whether it's getting worse and how much it disrupts your life: getting up once is a different story from breaking your sleep four times a night.

## An enlarged prostate: the usual suspect

The prostate is a gland roughly the size of a walnut that wraps around the first stretch of the urethra, the tube urine passes through on its way out. In many men it slowly grows with age, and that growth has nothing to do with cancer. It's called benign prostatic hyperplasia, or BPH. Because the gland surrounds the urethra, a bigger prostate can squeeze it like a foot on a garden hose, which is why the stream weakens and the bladder doesn't fully empty.

A few things worth knowing about BPH:

- It's benign: it isn't cancer and it doesn't turn into cancer.
- A bladder that routinely holds leftover urine raises the chance of infections and, over time, bladder stones or a day when urine won't come out at all.
- Treatments can ease the symptoms, from habit changes to medicines that relax the bladder outlet or shrink the gland; the right choice depends on each man's situation.

## Other causes that get a look

Not every weak stream or nighttime bathroom run comes from the prostate. Before assuming anything, the medical team weighs other possibilities:

- **A urinary or prostate infection:** often brings burning, cloudy or strong-smelling urine, and sometimes fever or pain low in the belly. If the urine test confirms it, it's treated during the visit; see our [urinary infections](/services/infecciones-urinarias) page for more.
- **Diabetes or blood sugar running high:** the body tries to flush extra glucose out through the urine, which shows up as peeing a lot, constant thirst and getting up at night. Sometimes the first clue to diabetes is noticed in the bathroom.
- **What you drink after dinner:** coffee, caffeinated sodas, tea and beer irritate the bladder and make the kidneys produce more urine.
- **Medications:** some blood pressure pills that remove fluid (diuretics), certain cold remedies with decongestants and some antihistamines can make the stream or the frequency worse. Don't stop them on your own; bring them to the visit.

## What a basic urology visit at the clinic covers

It starts with a conversation. You'll be asked how often you go during the day and at night, whether the stream has changed, when the dribbling began, what you drink with dinner and which medications you take. A three-day log of when you pee and what you drank helps a lot.

Depending on what you share, the next steps may include:

- **A urine test at the clinic:** it looks for signs of infection, blood you can't see with the naked eye, or sugar in the urine.
- **Blood work:** glucose to rule out diabetes and tests that show how well the kidneys are filtering, among others. It's drawn through our [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) service, and we reach out as soon as the lab sends the results back.
- **A physical exam and blood pressure check**, since blood pressure, weight and certain medications all affect how the bladder behaves.

If an infection is confirmed, you head home with your treatment in hand, because the medications prescribed during the visit are provided at the clinic. If things point to the prostate, you'll go over the options and how to tell whether they're working. And if you already live with diabetes or high blood pressure, the urinary symptoms become part of your [chronic conditions](/services/condiciones-cronicas) follow-up.

## The PSA test is a shared decision

PSA (prostate-specific antigen) is a protein measured in the blood. It can rise because of prostate cancer, but also because of an enlarged prostate, inflammation or a recent infection. That's why a high number isn't a diagnosis, and a normal one doesn't rule everything out.

The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) advises men aged 55 to 69 to make an individual decision about screening after talking through the possible benefits and the downsides, such as false results, extra procedures and treatment that may not have been needed. Having a father or brother with prostate cancer, or being Black, are details to bring up in that conversation. At the clinic, PSA is part of the [men's health](/services/salud-hombre) service and is ordered when you and the medical team decide on it together.

## When a urologist referral is arranged

Many cases are handled well with follow-up at the clinic. As part of the service, a referral to a urologist is arranged when a result or the way symptoms evolve calls for it, for example:

- A PSA that comes back high or keeps climbing from one test to the next.
- Blood in the urine, even if it happened only once.
- Symptoms that stay the same or get worse after trying treatment.
- Repeat urinary infections in a man, which deserve a closer look.

## Warning signs: go to the ER

These situations shouldn't wait for a visit on Airline Drive; get to the nearest emergency room or dial 911 when:

- You can't pee at all even though your bladder feels full, especially with pain or a hard, swollen lower belly.
- Your urine comes out red or has clots in it.
- You have a high fever with chills, pain in your side or lower back, or vomiting that keeps you from holding down fluids.
- You feel confused, very weak or you pass out.

## Habits that help starting tonight

While the cause is being sorted out, these often help:

1. Drink most of your water in the morning and afternoon, and cut back in the two hours before bed.
2. Try "double voiding" before bed: empty your bladder, wait a minute, then try again.
3. If you dribble at the end, wait a few extra seconds and press gently behind the scrotum, moving forward, to clear what's left in the urethra.
4. Don't hold it for hours in the truck or on the job site; plan stops even on a long route.
