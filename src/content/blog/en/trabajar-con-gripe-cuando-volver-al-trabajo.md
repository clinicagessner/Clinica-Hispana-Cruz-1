# Working With the Flu: When to Stay Home and Go Back

If you get paid by the day, calling in sick is a hard call. When you frame houses, clean office buildings overnight or work the line in a restaurant kitchen, a day at home can mean a day without a paycheck. That's why it's so common to see someone running a fever on a job site or sneezing over the prep table. The catch is that influenza rarely stops at one person: within a few days it can knock out half the crew, and the work stops anyway.

In this guide, the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz walks you through general CDC guidance on when to stay home, what to do if you truly have to work, and how to tell you're ready to go back without putting your coworkers at risk.

## Why the flu spreads so fast among coworkers

Flu travels mostly in the droplets people release when they cough, sneeze or talk up close, and also on hands that touch a contaminated surface and then reach the mouth, nose or eyes. Some workplaces stack all of those risks together:

- **On a construction site:** the shared truck ride in and out, one water cooler for the whole crew, tools passed hand to hand and lunch eaten shoulder to shoulder.
- **In cleaning jobs:** door handles, elevator buttons, railings and restrooms touched by dozens of people, often in closed rooms with little airflow.
- **In restaurants and kitchens:** heat, tight quarters and rush hour make it hard to keep your distance, and food and plates go through many hands before they reach a customer.

Something many people don't realize: someone with the flu can pass it on starting about a day before feeling sick, and is usually most contagious during the first days of symptoms. That's exactly when a lot of workers shrug it off as "just a cold" and keep going.

## How many days should you stay home? It's not a fixed number

The question we hear most often is "how many days do I need to miss?" Current CDC guidance for respiratory viruses doesn't give a set number of days. Instead, it uses two conditions that both have to be true:

1. **Your symptoms are getting better overall** (less coughing, less fatigue, fewer body aches).
2. **You've gone a full day without a fever and without taking fever-reducing medicine**, such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen.

For most healthy adults that takes several days, but every case is different. Watch out for the pill trap: if you take something for the fever in the morning and feel fine by lunch, that doesn't count. The fever has to be gone on its own.

If your job is physically demanding, think about safety too. Climbing scaffolding, hauling material or running equipment with a fever, dizziness or a weak body raises the risk of falls and injuries, and no day's pay is worth that.

## If you really have to work: how to cut down on spreading it

The best move is staying home while you have a fever. But if the fever is gone and you still have a cough, or your situation simply won't let you miss work, these steps help protect everyone else:

### Construction and outdoor jobs

- Wear a mask inside the truck and in enclosed spaces, and roll the windows down when several of you ride together.
- Bring your own water bottle and don't share cups, thermoses or cigarettes.
- Eat a little apart from the group, outdoors rather than inside the trailer or site office.
- Wipe down handles on tools other people use too.

### House, office and hotel cleaning

- Wear gloves and change them between areas, then wash your hands once they come off.
- Cough or sneeze into your elbow, never into the hand that's about to touch knobs or remotes.
- When you can, open a window or turn on the exhaust fan while cleaning bathrooms and small rooms.

### Restaurants, kitchens and food service

- Let your manager know before your shift starts: many kitchens have health rules about sick employees, and they may be able to move you to a task that doesn't involve handling food.
- Wash your hands with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, especially after coughing, blowing your nose or using the restroom.
- Don't taste food with shared utensils, and stay away from plated dishes while you still have symptoms.

On any job, a pack of tissues in your pocket and hand sanitizer within reach make more of a difference than you'd think.

## Back at work: the five days after you return

Even once you meet both conditions to go back, you may still be able to pass the virus on for a while. That's why the CDC recommends extra precautions for the five days after you return:

- Wear a mask when you're around other people indoors.
- Keep some distance at lunch and on rides to the job.
- Keep washing your hands often and covering your coughs.
- If the fever comes back or symptoms get worse, go home and start counting again.

It's normal for a cough to hang on longer than everything else. A mild cough that keeps easing isn't a reason to keep missing work, but a cough that's getting worse or leaves you short of breath needs a checkup.

## When it's worth stopping by the clinic

Not every cold needs a visit, but some situations call for getting checked soon:

- **High fever or feeling very sick in the first days.** Flu antivirals work best when started within the first two days of symptoms, and the medical team will decide whether they're right for you.
- **You need to know what you have.** The rapid flu test gives a result in minutes and helps guide treatment. If your throat really hurts, it could be strep, and that's what our [strep test](/services/prueba-strep) is for.
- **You have diabetes, high blood pressure or another chronic condition.** The flu can hit harder in those cases; if you already come in for [diabetes, blood pressure or cholesterol care](/services/condiciones-cronicas), mention it during the visit.
- **Symptoms improved and then came back worse**, or the cough has dragged on for many days.

## Warning signs: don't wait, go to the ER or call 911

Get emergency care right away if you or a coworker has:

- Trouble breathing or shortness of breath.
- Chest pain or pressure that stays even when you stop and rest.
- Bluish or gray lips or face.
- Confusion, extreme drowsiness or trouble waking up.
- Severe dizziness, fainting or seizures.
- Signs of dehydration: not urinating, very dry mouth, unable to keep fluids down.

On a hot job site or in a kitchen, fever plus dehydration can turn serious fast. If someone passes out at work, don't send them home behind the wheel: call 911.
