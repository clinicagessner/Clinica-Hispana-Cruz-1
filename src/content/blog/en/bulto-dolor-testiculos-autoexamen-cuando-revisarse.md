# Testicle Pain or a Lump: Self-Exam and When to Get Checked

It usually happens in the shower: your hand finds something that wasn't there before, a hard pea-sized bump or one side that feels heavier. Many men keep quiet and wait for it to go away. Others feel a dull ache in the scrotum after hauling material on a job site and blame the effort. Most of the time the cause is harmless, but two situations cannot wait, and knowing how to tell them apart changes the outcome.

In this article the medical team at Clínica Hispana Cruz explains how to check yourself at home, what each finding may mean and when it makes sense to have it looked at.

## First things first: pain you should not ride out at home

One situation comes before everything else. If a testicle suddenly starts to hurt badly, with no blow to explain it, it may be testicular torsion: the cord that carries blood to the testicle twists on itself and cuts off circulation. It is more common in teenagers and young men, yet it can happen at any age, even during sleep.

Clues that often come with it:

- The pain hits all at once, sometimes waking you up at night, and may spread to the groin or belly.
- Nausea or vomiting shows up along with the pain.
- That testicle sits higher than the other or seems turned sideways.
- The scrotum swells and turns red within a few hours.

With that picture, dial 911 or have someone drive you to the ER right away. Do not stop at the clinic first or wait to see whether it settles: the testicle can often be saved when the twist is corrected quickly, and every hour without blood flow lowers those odds. Pain that eases a little without treatment does not rule the problem out.

## How to do the self-exam

Checking once a month mainly helps you learn how your testicles normally feel, so a change stands out. You need no equipment:

1. Do it after a warm shower, when the skin of the scrotum is relaxed.
2. Stand in front of a mirror and look for swelling or a change in shape on either side.
3. Hold one testicle at a time with both hands: thumbs in front, index and middle fingers behind.
4. Roll it gently between your fingers, the way you would check a grape, feeling for hard spots, small bumps or areas that hurt when touched.
5. Find the epididymis, a soft cord-like tube that runs along the back and top of the testicle. It is normal, and many men mistake it for a lump.
6. Repeat on the other side and compare.

One testicle being slightly larger or hanging lower than the other is typical. What deserves attention is anything new, something that was not there last month.

## What the thing you found might be

Most findings are not cancer. This table sums up the most common ones, but it does not replace an exam:

| What you notice | What it often is | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Thick veins above the testicle, like a small bag of worms, more often on the left | Varicocele | Mention it at a routine visit |
| One side of the scrotum gets bigger, soft and nearly painless | Hydrocele, a buildup of fluid | Get it checked to confirm |
| Small, smooth bump above or behind the testicle | Epididymal cyst | Get it checked; it is almost always harmless |
| Pain that builds over a day or two, with warmth, swelling or burning when you pee | Inflammation from an infection (epididymitis) | See someone soon |
| Bulge in the groin that grows when you cough or lift and may drop into the scrotum | Inguinal hernia | Book a visit; ER if it gets stuck and hurts |
| Hard bump attached to the testicle, painless, not changing | A tumor needs to be ruled out | Visit within the next few days |

## The hard lump that does not hurt

This is the point most often misunderstood. Plenty of men assume that if it does not hurt it is not serious, and with testicular cancer the opposite tends to be true: the classic sign is a firm bump that causes no discomfort, sometimes with a heavy feeling in the scrotum. According to MedlinePlus, from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, it is an uncommon cancer that mostly affects men between 20 and 39, exactly the age when checkups are the last thing on anyone's mind. Having had a testicle that did not descend as a child, or a father or brother with this cancer, raises the risk.

The good news is that, caught early, it has very good odds of cure. That is why a new hard bump is worth checking even when it does not bother you, without letting months go by.

## What happens when you come in

During the [men's health](/services/salud-hombre) visit, the medical team starts by asking when you first noticed it, whether it has changed size, and whether there was a blow, a fever or burning when you pee. Then they examine the scrotum and groin, both standing and lying down, because some conditions, such as a varicocele or a hernia, are easier to feel while upright.

Depending on what they find, they may order:

- A urine test, if there is burning or a suspected infection. You can read more on the [urinary tract infections](/services/infecciones-urinarias) page.
- [Blood work](/services/examenes-sangre), when it helps point the case in the right direction.
- An imaging study of the scrotum: the visit is where the team weighs which study is needed based on the exam.

If the cause turns out to be an infection, you leave with the treatment the team chose and pick it up right there at the clinic's [pharmacy](/services/farmacia), with no extra stop on the way home. If the exam or the studies show something that needs a different kind of care, the plan includes specialist referral when results call for it.

## Other complaints worth bringing up

Not every ache in the scrotum comes from the testicle itself. Pain that starts in the lower back and moves down toward the groin can be a kidney stone. Discomfort after long hours behind the wheel or lifting heavy loads may come from a pulled muscle or a hernia that is just starting. If you notice something like that often, mention it at your visit instead of putting up with it.

## When not to wait

Besides a possible torsion, these situations call for the ER or 911, not a regular visit:

- A hard hit to the testicles with swelling that keeps growing, a large bruise or pain that does not ease within an hour.
- A hernia that used to slip back in by itself and is now firm, red and very painful.
- Chills and a high fever together with pain when peeing or in the scrotum.
- Feeling the urge to pee but nothing comes out.

## What you can do starting today

Set a monthly reminder on your phone for the self-exam, tied to rent day or payday, so it does not slip. If you find something new, write down the date, the side and roughly how big it is compared with something familiar, like a bean or a marble.
